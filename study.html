Study Guide

SRE & PE in 2026

What I would study if I were preparing for an SRE or Production Engineering interview today. Curated reading, opinions on what matters most, and the operational AI skills that are quietly becoming the biggest hiring lever in the field.

This is a working document. I keep it updated for friends and colleagues who ask "what should I read?" Each section opens with a short opinion on what actually matters in that area, then a tight list of resources I trust. The biggest rewrite in this round is the AI section: most SRE study guides on the internet treat AI as "knows how to call an LLM API," and that bar is now far too low.

Read it top to bottom or jump to the section you need. Every heading is its own URL, so sharing a single section is one click on the # next to the title.

01
Foundations

Foundations#

Foundations don't go out of date. Every SRE skill above this layer assumes you can read a kernel stack trace, reason about TCP retransmits, and write a small program that does what you meant. If you skip this layer, every other section becomes harder than it needs to be.

Pick one systems language deeply (Go is the modern default for new infrastructure code; Python remains the universal glue for tooling and automation) and learn enough of the other to be useful. Spend more time on Linux than feels reasonable. Networking is the most under-studied area among candidates I've talked to: knowing why DNS is slow today, why a TLS handshake added 200ms, or why packets are being dropped at the load balancer separates people who guess from people who diagnose.

Linux & systems

  • → Linux Journey. Free, well-paced introduction (now hosted on LabEx). Don't stop at the introductory tier; the networking and process-management sections are where the real value lives.
  • → Systems Performance, 2nd ed. (Brendan Gregg). The canonical reference. You won't read it cover to cover, but you'll keep coming back to it. The methodology chapters (USE method, RED method) alone are worth the cover price.
  • → Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective (Bryant & O'Hallaron). If you skipped systems in school, the labs in chapters 6 to 10 will close the gap faster than anything else I've found.
  • → "Linux Performance Tools" by Brendan Gregg. Forty minutes that will give you a real mental map of where to look when a box is slow.

Programming languages

  • → A Tour of Go. One afternoon. Then read Effective Go and write something real (a small CLI, a metrics scraper, a tiny proxy).
  • → 100 Go Mistakes and How to Avoid Them (Teiva Harsanyi). The book that turns a beginner into someone whose PRs senior engineers approve quickly. Goroutine leaks, context propagation, slice-sharing footguns.
  • → Fluent Python, 2nd ed. (Luciano Ramalho). Bridges the gap from "I can write Python" to "I write Python that other people enjoy reading." Type hints, dataclasses, async patterns.
  • → pytest and mypy, in anger. Most Python interview questions are actually questions about how disciplined you are. Bring tests and types to every code sample.

Networking

  • → Beej's Guide to Network Programming. Free, friendly, and the fastest way to internalize how sockets really work.
  • → High Performance Browser Networking (Ilya Grigorik). Free online. Don't let the "browser" in the title fool you; it's the best explanation of TCP, TLS, HTTP/2, and QUIC behavior I know.
  • → Practice with tcpdump, ss, dig, mtr. In an interview, "I'd tcpdump on the egress interface and look for retransmits" is the kind of answer that lands.
02
Production Systems

Production Systems#

Production systems are about what survives contact with reality: consistency tradeoffs, failure modes, the patterns that keep a service alive when things go sideways. The interview signal here is whether you reason from first principles ("what fails first under load, and what does the failure look like to the caller?") or from buzzwords ("just put a circuit breaker on it").

One bias: spend more time on resilience patterns than on system-design checklists. Anyone can list "load balancer, cache, database." Fewer candidates can explain why their retry policy needs jitter, why their write path needs idempotency keys, or why their cache invalidation strategy is going to bite them at 3 AM.

Core reading

  • → Designing Data-Intensive Applications (Kleppmann, 2nd ed. updates in flight). If you read one book on this list, this is the one. Replication, partitioning, consistency, batch vs. stream. Every chapter shows up in production design questions.
  • → Release It!, 2nd ed. (Michael Nygard). The book on resilience patterns. Timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads, idempotency. Older but the chapters on stability antipatterns age like wine.
  • → Site Reliability Engineering + The Site Reliability Workbook (Google). Free online. The principles chapters are canonical; the case-study chapters (especially "Postmortem Culture" and "Managing Load") are the parts you should actually be able to discuss in an interview.
  • → Database Internals (Alex Petrov). Reads like a sequel to DDIA, with much more on storage engines and replication protocols. Good preparation for any role touching a stateful service.
  • → Jepsen analyses (Kyle Kingsbury). Pick three from databases you've used. After you've seen a Jepsen report find a serializability bug, you'll never read a vendor's consistency claims the same way again.

Practice and pattern reps

  • → System Design Primer. Use it as a checklist of vocabulary, not a script. Reviewers can tell when someone is reciting it.
  • → Papers We Love: distributed systems. The original papers behind a lot of the textbook material. Read "Dynamo," "Spanner," and "Kafka: a Distributed Messaging System" at minimum.
  • → Amazon Builders' Library. Free articles by Amazon principal engineers. "Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter" and "Workload isolation using shuffle-sharding" are required reading.
03
Observability

Observability#

Observability is what separates engineers who guess from engineers who know. The discipline has consolidated hard in the last three years: OpenTelemetry is the floor (production adoption roughly doubled year over year through 2025, and vendor lock-in around metrics is now a deal-breaker for many buyers), SLOs are the lingua franca for "is this thing healthy enough?", and eBPF has gone from niche to mainstream for low-overhead production tracing.

For interviews, the signal is whether you can design instrumentation alongside the system, not bolt it on afterwards. "What metric would you alert on, and what's its SLI definition?" is the question to be ready for. "How would you debug this without adding more logs?" is the follow-up.

The OpenTelemetry world

  • → OpenTelemetry docs. Start with the collector and the language SDK for whatever you use most. Understand the data model (resources, attributes, scopes, signals) before the wire format.
  • → Honeycomb blog and Charity Majors. The clearest writing on what "observability" actually means versus traditional monitoring. The arguments for high-cardinality, wide events are foundational.
  • → Distributed Systems Observability (Cindy Sridharan). Free short O'Reilly report. The best ninety-page introduction you'll find.

SLOs & reliability math

  • → Implementing SLOs (SRE Workbook). Read this twice. The first time for the mechanics, the second time noticing how often the chapter says "this is a conversation, not a calculation."
  • → Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting. If you can explain why a 1-hour 14.4x burn-rate alert paired with a 6-hour 6x burn-rate alert beats a static "five 9s in the last 5 minutes" alert, you can pass most SRE alerting questions.

eBPF & live production debugging

  • → BPF Performance Tools (Brendan Gregg). Pair it with practicing bpftrace one-liners on a real Linux box. The mental model alone changes how you think about debugging.
  • → Isovalent learning resources. Free eBPF and Cilium tutorials. The "Getting started with eBPF" lab is the fastest hands-on path.

Incident response

  • → PagerDuty Incident Response. Public, opinionated playbook. Read it once; reread the postmortem sections before any leveling interview.
  • → Learning From Incidents. The community pushing past "five whys" toward genuine learning. "How Complex Systems Fail" by Richard Cook is the foundational essay.
04
Platform Engineering

Platform Engineering#

SRE has bifurcated. Half the role is still on-call and reliability work; the other half is "build the paved road that lets product teams ship safely without your help." That second half has a name now (Platform Engineering) and a recognizable shape: Kubernetes as the substrate, GitOps as the delivery model, internal developer platforms as the product, and cost control as a first-class concern.

The interview signal here is whether you can talk about tradeoffs. Anyone can list "we use ArgoCD." Fewer can articulate why they chose ArgoCD over Flux, what they gave up, and what they'd do differently next time. Same for service meshes: knowing when not to adopt one is more valuable than knowing how to install Istio.

Kubernetes, beyond the basics

  • → Kubernetes the Hard Way (Kelsey Hightower). Still the best one-weekend project for actually understanding how a cluster fits together. Do it on bare cloud VMs, not Minikube.
  • → Platform Engineering on Kubernetes (Mauricio Salatino). The right book if you want to think in terms of building a developer-facing platform on top of K8s rather than just running it.
  • → Operators & CRDs. Build a tiny operator with Kubebuilder. Even a 50-line controller teaches you more about the K8s API than weeks of kubectl.
  • → CNCF Landscape. Not for memorization. Use it once to see the shape of what exists, and as a reference when an interviewer name-drops something.

GitOps, CI/CD, supply chain

  • → ArgoCD or Flux. Pick one, deploy a real app with it, then read the docs for the other and form opinions on the differences.
  • → SLSA + Sigstore. Supply chain security has moved from "nice to have" to "required by procurement." Understand provenance, attestations, and how artifacts get signed end to end.

Infrastructure as code

  • → Terraform or Pulumi or OpenTofu. The choice matters less than your fluency with state management, module composition, and how you'd review someone else's IaC PR.
  • → Infrastructure as Code, 3rd ed. (Kief Morris). Tool-agnostic principles, which is what you actually need once you've outgrown copy-pasting tutorials.

FinOps

  • → FinOps Foundation framework. The shared vocabulary. Once GPUs entered the picture, cost attribution and right-sizing stopped being optional skills for senior SREs.
  • → Container Security (Liz Rice). Not strictly FinOps but adjacent: knowing what's running where and with what privileges is the prerequisite to knowing what you can safely turn off.
05
AI & ML Ops

AI & ML Ops#

This is the section I rewrote from scratch. Two years ago "AI for SRE" meant knowing how to call an LLM API and maybe explaining what a vector database was. That bar is now far too low. The reality is that SRE owns serving, observability, cost, and reliability for AI workloads in most organizations that ship them. The skill gap here is the biggest hiring lever in the field in 2026, and the candidates who close it stand out immediately.

The shape of this skill set: you understand inference serving well enough to debug a vLLM pod that's dropping tokens; you can read an OpenTelemetry GenAI trace and tell whether your retrieval step or your model step is the bottleneck; you know how GPU scheduling on Kubernetes actually works when the cluster has heterogeneous hardware; you can design evals for an agent system instead of waving your hands about "hallucinations"; and you treat prompt injection with the same seriousness you treat SQL injection.

LLM inference serving

vLLM is the operational default for self-hosted inference. The two ideas to internalize: PagedAttention (the KV cache is paged like virtual memory, which is why fragmentation drops from roughly 30% to under 2% and throughput jumps several-fold) and continuous batching (requests join and leave the batch every iteration instead of waiting for a full batch to complete). If you understand those two, you can reason about most of the metrics you'll see in production.

The metrics that matter operationally: time-to-first-token (TTFT), inter-token latency (ITL), vllm:num_requests_waiting (queue depth), vllm:gpu_cache_usage (how full the paged KV cache is), and tokens per second per GPU. p99 TTFT on H100s under real load is typically in the low hundreds of milliseconds; if you're seeing seconds, something is wrong with batching or memory.

  • → vLLM documentation. Start with the architecture page and the production-deployment guide. The vLLM blog has deep posts on PagedAttention and disaggregated prefill/decode.
  • → Know the alternatives. TensorRT-LLM (highest throughput on NVIDIA hardware, painful to operate), SGLang (rising fast, especially for structured output), llama.cpp (the right answer when "ship to a laptop" is the requirement), TGI from Hugging Face (less common in 2026 but you'll meet it).

GPU operations on Kubernetes

The default NVIDIA device plugin gives you whole-GPU allocation, which is wasteful for anything except the largest models. The toolkit to know: MIG (Multi-Instance GPU, hardware partitioning on A100/H100), MPS (Multi-Process Service, software-level GPU sharing), and time-slicing (lowest isolation, fine for dev). On top of that, the NVIDIA KAI Scheduler (open-sourced in 2025) brings fractional GPU allocation, topology-aware placement, and gang scheduling to Kubernetes. Real workloads have moved utilization from the 10 to 15% range up to 35 to 40% with proper scheduling.

  • → NVIDIA Cloud Native docs. Device plugin, GPU operator, MIG configuration. The reference material the rest of the ecosystem assumes you've read.
  • → KAI Scheduler (and the academic "Gang Scheduling for Deep Learning" background). Fractional GPUs, gang admission, hierarchical queues. Even if you use a different scheduler, the concepts apply.

LLM observability (the 2026 standard)

The OpenTelemetry community shipped GenAI semantic conventions that are now the lingua franca. Spans are invoke_agent at the top, chat for each LLM call, and execute_tool for tool invocations. Attributes capture model name, token counts (gen_ai.usage.input_tokens, gen_ai.usage.output_tokens), finish reason, and optionally the full prompt and completion when you've opted into that. Cost falls out of the token counts multiplied by per-model pricing. Every major observability vendor now ships native support.

  • → OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions. Read the spec once. It's short, and it's what every framework's instrumentation now emits.
  • → "Observability Trends for 2026: GenAI and OpenTelemetry" (Elastic). Concise industry snapshot: who's adopting what, where the gaps are.

RAG & vector databases

The interesting questions are no longer "what is RAG" but "what's your retrieval eval strategy?" and "why pgvector instead of a dedicated vector DB?" The honest answer for many workloads is that Postgres + pgvector is enough until it isn't, and the discipline of measuring retrieval quality (NDCG, MRR, hit-rate on a held-out eval set) matters far more than which vector store you picked.

  • → pgvector documentation. The defaults are surprisingly good. Understand HNSW vs. IVFFlat indexes before you reach for something fancier.
  • → Ragas for RAG evals. Faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision. The discipline of measuring is half the value.
  • → Embedding model choice is a real tradeoff. OpenAI's text-embedding-3 is the easy default; local models like bge-large or nomic-embed-text are competitive and cheaper. Benchmark with your own corpus, not the leaderboards.

Agent systems & evals

Agent reliability is mostly the discipline of evals: a labeled set of tool-use scenarios, a harness that runs them on every change, and clear pass/fail criteria. The failure modes you need to handle gracefully are well-catalogued by now: hallucinated tool calls, invalid parameters, infinite retry loops, tools that succeed silently with the wrong result.

  • → LangSmith or Braintrust. Hosted eval platforms. Pick one to learn the shape of the workflow; the patterns transfer.
  • → Anthropic cookbook and OpenAI cookbook. Concrete patterns for tool use, structured output, and evals. Read them as collections of working examples, not as gospel.

Safety, guardrails, prompt injection

Prompt injection is the new SQL injection: a well-understood attack class with imperfect defenses. Defense in depth is the only honest approach: input filtering, output validation, per-user rate limits on both requests and tokens, sandboxed tool execution, and a logging story that makes detection possible after the fact.

  • → OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications. The shared vocabulary for this problem space. Prompt injection, insecure output handling, sensitive information disclosure.
  • → Llama Guard and similar moderation models. Useful as one layer; not a complete defense on their own.
  • → Simon Willison on prompt injection. Years of clear-eyed writing on what works, what doesn't, and what's still an open problem.

The bigger picture

  • → AI Engineering (Chip Huyen). The current best book-length treatment of LLM applications from a systems perspective. Read it cover to cover; it's worth the time.
  • → Hugging Face blog. The pulse of what's actually shipping in open-source ML. Their deployment posts are particularly good.
06
Interview Strategy

Interview Strategy#

SRE and PE interviews are not LeetCode loops. The strongest signal is operational thinking: do you reach for the right metric, the right tool, the right question? A mid-level loop typically has a coding round (more "parse this log file" than "invert a binary tree"), a system design round with an operational lens (capacity, SLOs, failure modes), a debugging or troubleshooting round on a Linux box or in a small simulated system, and a behavioral round that usually centers on a recent incident.

Level distinguishes itself in the behavioral and design rounds, not the coding round. At senior, you should be able to walk through an incident with a clear narrative arc: detection, response, recovery, what you'd change, what you learned, what surprised you. At staff and above, you should be able to describe systems you've shaped, not just operated, and the tradeoffs you steered through.

Prep resources

  • → SRE Interview Prep Guide (mxssl). Comprehensive, well-maintained checklist. Useful for spotting your own blind spots, less useful as a sequential study plan.
  • → Splunk's SRE interview prep guide. Realistic prompts. Practice answering aloud; the difference between knowing and explaining is large.
  • → System Design Interview vol. 1 & 2 (Alex Xu). Worthwhile for vocabulary and pacing. Treat the answers as one possible take, not the answer.
  • → Interviewing.io or Pramp. Mock interviews with real engineers. Five mocks will teach you more about your gaps than fifty hours of reading.

What to practice out loud

  • → Your incident story. Pick one. Be able to tell it in two minutes and in ten. Practice both.
  • → "Walk me through a slow request." From client TCP handshake through DNS, TLS, load balancer, application, database, and back. Know where to look at each layer.
  • → "Design an SLO for this service." Be ready to define an SLI, pick a target, and discuss how you'd alert on the burn rate.
  • → For AI-touching roles: "We're serving a Llama 3 70B model and p99 TTFT just doubled. What do you check first?" If you have an answer ready, you're in a small group.
